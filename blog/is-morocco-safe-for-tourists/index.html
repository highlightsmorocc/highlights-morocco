Is Morocco safe for tourists?

Honest answers on scams, petty theft, solo women, driving and health, with practical tips from a local Marrakech tour operator.

A practical guide to travelling safely in Morocco: the real risks, how to avoid them, emergency numbers and what a private tour changes.

The short answer

Yes, Morocco is generally safe for tourists, and it welcomes millions of visitors every year. The usual problems are small: petty theft in crowded places, persistent touts, unofficial guides and prices that change after the service. You can avoid almost all of them by agreeing prices first, keeping valuables out of sight, using registered taxis or a private driver and following local dress customs. Check your own government's travel advice before you travel.

No country is risk free, so this guide is honest about what can go wrong and how to prevent it. If you prefer to have a local team beside you, message us on WhatsApp and we will explain how our private tours work.

Violent crime against tourists is unusual, but it is wise to stay alert, especially at night and in crowded places.

Common scams and how to say no

  • "The road is closed" story. A stranger says the street ahead is closed and offers to take you somewhere else, often a shop. Check the route on your map.
  • Unofficial guide in the medina. Someone starts to walk with you and asks for money at the end. Say clearly that you do not need a guide.
  • Price after the service. For a henna, a photo with animals or a ride, agree the price before you start.
  • Taxi without a meter. Ask for the meter, or agree the price before you enter. Your riad can tell you the usual fare.
  • "Free" gifts. A bracelet or a handful of mint placed in your hand, followed by a request for payment. You can hand it back.

A smile and a firm "la, shukran" (no, thank you) is usually enough. Moroccans are used to tourists, and most people you meet are friendly and honest.

Is Morocco safe for solo female travellers?

Many women travel alone in Morocco, and many have a great trip. The most common problem is unwanted attention or comments, especially in busy medinas. These tips help:

  • Dress modestly: covered shoulders and knees are more comfortable in cities and villages.
  • Walk with purpose, and avoid empty streets after dark.
  • Use registered taxis, or a private driver for longer routes.
  • Book your first nights in advance and share your plans with someone you trust.
  • Choose a private tour or a local guide for the first days if you want more peace of mind.

Health, water and the desert

  • Water: most visitors drink bottled water.
  • Sun and heat: summer in Marrakech and the Sahara is very hot. Spring and autumn are more comfortable, and you can read our best time to visit Morocco.
  • Desert trips: carry water, wear a scarf and a hat, and follow your guide on camels, quads and buggies. See our Sahara excursion guide.
  • Insurance: take travel insurance that covers medical care and the activities you plan to do.

Is Morocco safe for tourists: frequently asked questions

Is Morocco safe for tourists?

Yes, Morocco is generally considered safe for tourists, and it welcomes millions of visitors every year. The most common problems are petty theft, persistent touts and overcharging, not violent crime. Use common sense, keep valuables out of sight and check your government's travel advice before you go.

What are the most common scams in Morocco?

Typical scams include a stranger who "helps" you find your way and then asks for money, an unofficial guide in the medina, prices agreed after the service, taxis without a meter and a "closed road" story to take you to a shop. A firm, polite "no, thank you" and agreeing prices first solve most of them.

Is Morocco safe for solo female travellers?

Many women travel alone in Morocco without problems, but unwanted attention and comments are common, mainly in the busy medinas. Dress modestly, walk with confidence, avoid empty streets at night, use registered taxis or a private driver and book accommodation in advance. A private tour with a local guide can make the trip easier.

Is it safe to walk around the medina at night?

The busy parts of Marrakech and Fes are lively in the evening, and many visitors walk there. The narrow, empty alleys are another matter: stay on the main streets, avoid carrying valuables and, if you are not sure of the way, ask your riad to arrange a person to meet you or use a taxi.

Is the tap water safe to drink in Morocco?

Most visitors drink bottled water, which is cheap and easy to find. Avoid ice in drinks if you are not sure of the source, and wash or peel fresh fruit and vegetables. Fresh orange juice and food from busy stalls are usually fine.

Is it safe to drive in Morocco?

Driving is possible, but traffic in the cities can be chaotic, mountain roads are long and winding, and police checkpoints are common. Many travellers prefer a private driver for long routes such as Marrakech to Merzouga, so they can rest and enjoy the landscape.

What emergency numbers should I know in Morocco?

The police number is 19 in cities, the Gendarmerie Royale number is 177 outside cities, and the ambulance and fire service (Civil Protection) number is 15. Keep the address of your hotel in writing and the phone number of your tour operator.

Is the Sahara desert safe for tourists?

Yes, when you travel with an organised tour. Desert camps near Merzouga are used to tourists. The main risks are heat, sun and dehydration, so carry water, wear a hat and light clothes and follow your guide, especially on quad, buggy and camel trips.

Do I need travel insurance for Morocco?

Travel insurance is a good idea for any trip, to cover medical care, cancellations and lost items. Check that your policy covers activities such as quad, buggy and camel rides if you plan to do them.

How does a private tour help me stay safe in Morocco?

With a private tour you travel with your own driver and a local guide, you do not need to negotiate taxis or find your way in the medinas, and you have a person to call. This removes most of the common problems for first-time visitors.
