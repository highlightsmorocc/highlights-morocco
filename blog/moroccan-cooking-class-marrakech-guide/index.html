The short answer

A Moroccan cooking class in Marrakech is a hands-on class where a local cook teaches you to prepare a tagine and other traditional dishes, using local spices and fresh ingredients, and you sit down at the end to eat what you cooked. No experience is needed, and the class suits couples, families and food lovers. The price is on request and depends on your group, and we confirm the place, the time and the menu with you before you book.

Moroccan food is about patience and spices. In a cooking class you learn how a tagine is built, how spices are balanced and how simple ingredients become a meal worth sharing. It is also one of the best ways to understand Moroccan culture, and a relaxed plan for a rest day between tours. This guide explains how a class works, what you cook, the spices you will meet, who it suits, what to bring and how to book. When you are ready, message us on WhatsApp for a clear price.

What you cook in a Marrakech cooking class

The star of the class is the tagine, a slow-cooked stew that takes its name from the conical clay pot in which it is made. Typical tagines combine meat or vegetables with onions, spices, olives or preserved lemon, and the flavours build slowly as the pot cooks. Besides the tagine, you can expect other traditional dishes, such as fresh salads, bread and a dessert, and the menu is confirmed when you contact us. We adapt it to diets and allergies. For more on the dishes, read our Moroccan food guide.

  • Tagine: the classic slow-cooked dish, in a clay pot.
  • Salads: small cooked and fresh vegetable salads that start the meal.
  • Bread: the daily bread that Moroccans eat with every dish.
  • Couscous: a traditional dish, often served on Fridays.
  • Mint tea: the drink that closes the meal.

The spices you will learn about

Spices are the heart of Moroccan cooking. In the class you learn what each one does and how to balance them, so that you can cook with confidence at home.

  • Cumin: warm and earthy, used in many tagines and salads.
  • Ginger and turmeric: add colour and a mild heat to stews.
  • Paprika: gives colour and a soft flavour.
  • Cinnamon: sweet warmth, common with meat and in desserts.
  • Saffron: a fine spice used in special dishes.
  • Ras el hanout: a blend of many spices, a signature of Moroccan cuisine.
  • Fresh herbs: coriander and parsley are added at the end.

Who a cooking class is for

  • Couples: a relaxed shared activity with a meal at the end.
  • Families: many families cook together; tell us the ages of the children.
  • Food lovers: learn techniques and tips that you can use at home.
  • Solo travellers: a friendly way to meet people and learn about the culture.
  • Anyone who needs a rest day: a calm plan between tours.

How a tagine is cooked: the basics

A tagine is cooked slowly, over a low heat, and this is the main lesson of the class. The clay pot has a conical lid that keeps the steam inside, so the food cooks in its own juices and stays tender. In a typical method, the cook layers onions and spices at the bottom, adds the meat or vegetables, adds a little water or stock and lets the pot simmer for a long time. Fresh herbs, olives or preserved lemon are added near the end. Because a tagine needs little water and little attention, it is a practical way to cook, and it works with a simple stove at home.

  • Low heat: the secret of a tender tagine is patience.
  • Layers: onions at the bottom protect the meat from burning.
  • The lid: keep it closed so the steam stays inside.
  • Balance: spices should support the dish, not hide it.
  • Resting: a short rest before serving helps the flavours settle.

Moroccan table manners

The class often ends with a shared meal, so it helps to know a few customs. In many homes, the tagine is served in the centre and everyone eats from the part of the dish in front of them. Moroccans use bread to pick up the food, and they eat with the right hand when they do not use cutlery. It is polite to wash your hands before the meal, to wait for the host to start and to say thank you to the cook. A meal ends with mint tea, and it is polite to accept at least one glass. These details are part of what makes a class a cultural experience, and your host will explain what to do.

Private or shared class?

We arrange the class for your group, so you can choose the time and adapt the menu. Private classes are a good choice for families, couples and friends, because the cook can give you full attention and adapt to your pace. Tell us how many people join and we confirm the details and the price.

What to bring and wear

  • Your appetite: the class ends with a full meal.
  • Comfortable clothes: clothes that you do not mind getting a little messy.
  • Closed shoes: a kitchen floor can be wet or hot.
  • A notebook: if you want to write down the recipes.
  • Your camera: for the dishes and the spices.
  • Questions: ask the cook about ingredients and techniques.

Diets and allergies

Tell us about any diet or allergy in advance. Vegetarian and vegan menus are easy to plan, because Moroccan cuisine has many vegetable dishes. For allergies, such as nuts, gluten or seafood, give us as much detail as you can, and we confirm the menu with the team before the class.

When to book a cooking class

A class works at any time of the year. Many travellers choose it for the middle of the day, as a break from the heat in summer, or for the evening, to end the day with dinner. On a rainy day in winter, it is a good indoor plan. Book as soon as you know your dates, especially in busy periods such as spring and autumn. Check Marrakech weather to choose the right moment.

Buying spices and a tagine pot in Marrakech

After the class, many travellers want to buy what they used. In the souks you find spice stalls with pyramids of colour, and shops that sell clay tagine pots and tea sets. Ask your cook which spices to buy and in what quantity, because small amounts are usually enough. For a tagine pot, check that it is made for cooking, not only for decoration, because decorative pots are often painted and not suitable for the stove. A cooking pot is plain, unglazed on the outside or lightly glazed, and heavy. Bargaining is part of shopping in Morocco, so do it with a smile, and compare a few shops before you buy. If you plan to shop for rugs or other crafts too, read our Moroccan rugs guide.

What you take home

You leave with new skills, a full stomach and often some recipes. Many travellers buy spices, a tagine pot and tea in the souks afterwards and cook the same dishes at home. If you want help choosing a good tagine pot or spices, ask your cook. A cooking class is also a gift idea for someone who loves food.
